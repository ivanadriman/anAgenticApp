# A General Guide to Checking Whether Sources Support a Text

The guide has three layers: what each citation is, whether it matches the claim, and whether the argument as a whole holds. Each question comes with its reasoning, so you can adapt the questions to cases they don't cover.

---

## Layer 1: What Is This Source?

| Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| What type of source is it (primary study, meta-analysis, review, theory paper, preprint, news, encyclopedia)? | Type sets the ceiling on what it can support. A theory paper can justify a hypothesis, not a finding. A preprint hasn't been through peer review. |
| Do the authors, journal, year, and DOI match the actual paper? | Mismatched metadata often signals a wrong or unchecked citation, especially in AI-assisted or auto-generated reference lists. |
| Is it independent, or does it rest on the same underlying work as your other sources? | Five citations that trace to one study are one piece of evidence, not five. |
| Who produced it, and could they have an interest in the conclusion? | This doesn't invalidate a source. It tells you where to look harder. |

---

## Layer 2: Does the Source Support This Specific Claim?

| Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Does it measure or discuss the exact thing claimed?** | The most common failure is a neighboring construct, such as perfectionism standing in for materialism, or purchase intention standing in for attachment. |
| **Is the supporting passage the paper's finding or its framing?** | Introductions and abstracts often state background assumptions or motivating hypotheses. A quote from that material isn't evidence the study demonstrated it. |
| **Does the design justify the verb?** | See the verb ladder below. |
| **Who or what was studied?** | Undergraduates, one country, one industry, or one period limit generalization. Check whether the claim's scope is wider than the sample's. |
| **How large and how certain is the effect?** | "Significant" is not "large." Look at effect sizes, intervals, heterogeneity, and publication-bias checks in meta-analyses. |
| **What do the authors say are their limitations?** | Authors usually flag the weaknesses a citing text drops. |
| **Does the source say the same thing in context?** | A quote can be accurate and still reversed or narrowed by the surrounding sentences. |

### The Verb Ladder

Match the strength of the claim to the design.

| Design | Supports language like | Does not support |
|---|---|---|
| Cross-sectional survey | "is associated with," "relates to" | "causes," "drives," "reduces" |
| Longitudinal or cohort trend | "precedes," "coincides with," "predicts" | "produces," without ruling out confounds |
| Statistical mediation on correlational data | "is consistent with a pathway through" | "works through," "directly mitigates" |
| Randomized experiment | "causes," "increases," "reduces" (in that setting) | Broad real-world generalization |
| Meta-analysis | Average pooled associations or effects | Mechanism, unless the included studies tested it |
| Theory or conceptual paper | "proposes," "argues," "hypothesizes" | "shows," "demonstrates" |

---

## Layer 3: Does the Whole Argument Hold?

| Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Is any link in the chain supplied by the author rather than a source?** | Arguments often cite A→B and B→C, then assert A→C as if cited. Mark which links have evidence and which are inference. |
| **Is the quote doing evidentiary or decorative work?** | A well-chosen quotation from an authoritative paper can create a sense of support for a claim the paper never makes. |
| **Are rival explanations acknowledged?** | A text that presents one mechanism as "the" explanation may be omitting alternatives the literature takes seriously. |
| **Do the sources span disciplines and contexts, or one tradition and one region?** | Consistent support from one subfield or Western student samples is weaker than convergence across fields and cultures. |
| **Does the conclusion claim more than the strongest single source?** | A conclusion should rarely exceed its best-supported premise. |

---

## Common Failure Patterns

1. **Adjacent-construct substitution.** The source studies something related, and the text treats it as the target.
2. **Correlation-to-causation drift.** "Associated with" becomes "drives" between source and summary.
3. **Framing-as-finding.** An introduction's assumption is cited as a result.
4. **Scope inflation.** A finding about one sample becomes a statement about "modern societies" or "humans."
5. **Orphaned quotation.** The quote is accurate, and the sentence it supports is not.
6. **Citation laundering.** Weak sources (encyclopedias, press releases) sit among strong ones and borrow their credibility.
7. **Mismatched or fabricated metadata.** The paper may exist, but not as listed.

---

## A Quick-Audit Workflow

1. **Triage (about 2 minutes per source).** Read the abstract. Ask about type, topic match, design, and sample.
2. **Locate the support.** Find where in the paper the claim appears. If you can't, treat it as unsupported until shown otherwise.
3. **Check the verb.** Compare the text's wording to the verb ladder above.
4. **Mark the inference links.** Draw the argument as a chain and label each link as *sourced* or *author-supplied*.
5. **Spend depth where the weight is.** Examine the one or two sources the argument leans on most, rather than every citation equally.
6. **Record confidence.** For each citation, note *verified*, *plausible but unchecked*, or *unsupported*. Don't merge the middle category into the first.

---

## Casual Summary

For each source, ask what it really studied, how strong the method is, and whether the sentence citing it says more than the paper does. Accurate quotes can still sit under claims the authors never made, so check the topic, the design, the wording of the verb, and which steps in the argument no source supports.
